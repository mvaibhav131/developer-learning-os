Every candidate hits a question they cannot answer. Interviewers know this, and they often ask hard questions on purpose, to see **how you react**. Bluffing is the worst response, because experienced interviewers can see it. Silence is the second worst. The best response is honest, calm and active: you say what you know, think aloud, and explain how you would find the rest.

> **Hinglish mein:** Jawab nahi aata? Ghabrao mat, jhooth bhi mat bolo. Seedha bolo "I haven't worked with that directly, but..." phir jo tum jaante ho wo batao, aur socho ki tum kaise pata karoge.

## A five-step plan

| Step | What to do | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1. Pause | Take a breath. Do not rush. | (Two seconds of silence is fine.) |
| 2. Be honest | Say what you do not know | I haven't worked with Kafka directly. |
| 3. Share related knowledge | Show what you do know | But I've used RabbitMQ for message queues. |
| 4. Think aloud | Reason from what you know | Let me think it through. I guess it works like this... |
| 5. Say how you would learn | Show your attitude | I would read the documentation and build a small example. |

## Weak and strong answers

| Weak | Strong |
| --- | --- |
| (Silence for ten seconds) | That's a good question. Let me think for a moment. |
| I don't know. | I'm not sure, but I think it works like this... |
| Yes, I know it. (and then guess) | I've used it only a little. Let me explain what I know. |
| It's not in my syllabus. | I haven't used that yet, but I'd like to learn it. |
| That's a wrong question. | Could you explain what you mean by that? |

## Phrases

```phrases
That's a good question. Let me think about it for a moment.
I haven't worked with that directly, but I know something similar.
I'm not completely sure, but my understanding is that...
Let me think aloud, and please correct me if I'm wrong.
Could you give me a small hint?
If I had to find the answer, I would check the documentation and test it.
Can I come back to this question later?
```

## In coding interviews

- **Think aloud.** The interviewer wants to hear your reasoning, not only the final code.
- Ask a **clarifying question**: "Can the array be empty?" "Are the numbers always positive?"
- Start with a **simple solution**, even if it is slow: "The brute force approach is... Its complexity is... Can I improve it?"
- If you are stuck, say it and ask for a hint: "I'm stuck on this part. Could you give me a hint?"

## What not to do

1. Do not invent an answer. A confident wrong answer is worse than an honest "I'm not sure".
2. Do not apologise again and again.
3. Do not blame the question, the syllabus or the interviewer.
4. Do not go silent for a long time. Say what you are thinking.

## Practice

1. Choose a topic you do not know well, for example Kafka, GraphQL or Kubernetes.
2. Imagine the question "How does it work?" Answer with the five steps above for one minute.
3. Record it. Listen. Did you pause, say the truth, link to something you know and say how you would learn?
4. Repeat with a second topic.

## Recap

- Pause, be honest, share related knowledge, think aloud, say how you would learn.
- Ask for a hint and clarify the question.
- Never bluff. Never freeze in silence.
- Interviewers value honesty and a learning attitude.
