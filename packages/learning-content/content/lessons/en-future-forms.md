In English there is no single "future tense" you must use. Native speakers choose between **will**, **going to**, the **present continuous** and the **present simple**, depending on what they mean. At work this choice shows whether you made a plan or just decided now, and whether you are promising something or only guessing.

> **Hinglish mein:** Hindi mein "main kal aaunga" kaafi hai. English mein ek hi baat ko alag-alag tareeke se bol sakte hain: jaisi aapki soch hai (turant decision, pehle se plan, ya fixed appointment), waisa form chuno.

## Will: decisions now, promises, offers

Use **will** (short form **'ll**) when you decide at the moment of speaking, make a promise or an offer, or give an opinion about the future.

- "The build is red." "I'll check it now." (decision now)
- I'll send you the report by 5 pm. (promise)
- I'll help you with the setup. (offer)
- I think the release will go well. (opinion)

Negative: **will not (won't)**. Question: **Will you review this?**

## Going to: plans and clear evidence

Use **be going to + base verb** for a plan you already made, or when you can see signs that something will happen.

- I'm going to refactor the payment module this sprint. (plan)
- We're going to move to a new database next quarter. (plan)
- Look at the logs. The server is going to crash. (evidence)

## Present continuous: arranged plans

When you have a time, a place or a person fixed, use the **present continuous**.

- I'm meeting the client at 4 pm.
- We're deploying on Friday night.
- She's flying to Bangalore tomorrow.

## Present simple: timetables

Use the present simple for fixed schedules, such as meetings, trains and releases.

- The meeting starts at 10 am.
- The sprint ends on Friday.

## Quick guide

| What you mean | Use | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Decision right now | will | OK, I'll restart the server. |
| Promise or offer | will | I'll get this done by Friday. |
| Plan made earlier | going to | I'm going to write the tests tomorrow. |
| Fixed arrangement | present continuous | I'm meeting the team at 3. |
| Timetable | present simple | The call starts at 11. |
| Possibility | might / may | It might take two days. |

## After if, when and before

After **if, when, before, after, as soon as, until** use the **present simple** for the future, not will.

- If the tests pass, **I will merge** the code. (not "if the tests will pass")
- I will call you when I **finish** the task. (not "when I will finish")
- We will start the deployment as soon as the approval **arrives**.

## Sentences for deadlines and plans

```phrases
I'll check the logs and get back to you.
I'm going to finish the API changes today.
We're deploying to staging at four o'clock.
I might need one more day for testing.
If the tests pass, I will merge the pull request.
I'll send you the update by the end of the day.
```

## Common mistakes

| Wrong | Correct |
| --- | --- |
| Tomorrow I will going to office. | Tomorrow I'm going to the office. |
| If it will fail, call me. | If it fails, call me. |
| I will meeting the client at 4. | I'm meeting the client at 4. |
| I am going to to fix it. | I'm going to fix it. |

## Practice

Choose the best form.

1. "Nobody answers the phone." "I (call) him again." Answer: I'll call him again.
2. We (release) on Friday. We already told the client. Answer: We're releasing on Friday.
3. The test (start) at 9 am tomorrow. Answer: starts.
4. If it (rain), I (work) from home. Answer: rains, will work.

Write three promises you can make to your team this week using I'll. Say them aloud.

## Recap

- Will: quick decisions, promises, offers, opinions.
- Going to: plans and evidence.
- Present continuous: arranged plans. Present simple: timetables.
- After if, when and before, use the present simple, not will.
