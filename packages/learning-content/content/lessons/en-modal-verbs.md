Modal verbs are small helper words that change the meaning of the main verb: ability, permission, advice, obligation and possibility. They are essential for polite requests and for talking about rules and risks in a team. They follow three simple grammar rules, so once you know those, you can use them with any verb.

> **Hinglish mein:** Modals ka matlab hai "sakta hoon / chahiye / zaroori hai / shayad". Teen rules yaad rakho: (1) modal ke baad verb ka simple form, (2) "to" nahi, (3) he/she ke saath "-s" nahi.

## Three grammar rules

1. After a modal comes the **base verb**: She can code. (not "She can codes")
2. No "to" after a modal (except have to, need to, ought to): You should test it. (not "You should to test")
3. No -s with he, she, it: He must leave. (not "He musts")

Negatives: cannot (can't), should not (shouldn't), must not (mustn't). Questions: swap the order. **Can you join? Should we merge?**

## What each modal does

| Meaning | Modal | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Ability | can, could | I can write SQL. She could read at four. |
| Permission | can, may, could | Can I join the call? May I ask a question? |
| Polite request | could, would | Could you share the logs? Would you mind reviewing this? |
| Advice | should, ought to | You should add tests. |
| Strong rule | must, have to | We have to get approval. You must not share passwords. |
| Possibility | might, may, could | It might be a cache issue. |
| Offer or promise | will, shall | I'll help. Shall I schedule a call? |

## Politeness ladder

The more modal-like words, the more polite the request.

| Level | Example |
| --- | --- |
| Direct (colleagues you know well) | Can you review this? |
| Polite | Could you review this? |
| Very polite or formal | Would it be possible to get a review today? |

## Must, have to and mustn't

- **must**: a strong rule or your own strong opinion. You must not commit passwords.
- **have to**: a rule from outside. We have to finish before the release.
- **don't have to**: not necessary. You don't have to attend the meeting. (optional)
- **mustn't**: forbidden. You mustn't share customer data.

**Don't have to** and **mustn't** have very different meanings. Learn them as a pair.

## Possibility and advice at work

```phrases
Could you share the error logs, please?
Would it be possible to extend the deadline by a day?
You should restart the service and check again.
We have to fix this before the release.
It might be a network problem.
You don't have to join the call if you are busy.
I can take this ticket.
```

## Common mistakes

| Wrong | Correct |
| --- | --- |
| She cans code. | She can code. |
| I can able to join. | I can join. / I am able to join. |
| You should to test it. | You should test it. |
| He must to go now. | He must go now. |
| We should to add tests. | We should add tests. |
| Can you please to help me? | Could you please help me? |

## Practice

Rewrite each sentence to sound polite.

1. Give me the file. Answer: Could you send me the file, please?
2. Reply today. Answer: Would it be possible to reply today?
3. Fix this bug. Answer: Could you take a look at this bug?

Now choose the right word: You (mustn't / don't have to) attend the optional demo. Answer: don't have to.

## Recap

- Modal + base verb. No to. No -s.
- Could and would make requests more polite than can and will.
- Should = advice. Must and have to = rule. Might = possible.
- Don't have to = not necessary. Mustn't = forbidden.
